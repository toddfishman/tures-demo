Strategic brief

An AI concierge that books the trip you describe.

Not researches one for you.

The wedge

Tures is an executor, not a search engine: you describe the trip in plain English, it books every leg — flight by flight, hotel by hotel, dinner by dinner — to your exact specs, and tells you the truth when reality doesn't cooperate.

Every other "AI travel" product still ends with a list of links the human has to click. Tures ends with confirmation numbers in your inbox.

Who is Andy Rockwell?

Not "the luxury traveler" — that's a marketing segment. Andy is a specific person:

  • Mid-to-late 30s, $300K+ HHI, in finance, tech, law, or healthcare. Travels 12–25 personal nights a year, plus business.
  • Knows hotels by name. He didn't ask for "a boutique hotel in Paris." He asked for Real Christine. He built the Lapland leg around Javri Lodge.
  • Status traveler. Airline status, Amex Platinum or Centurion, maybe a Virtuoso advisor he tolerates more than likes.
  • Friction-allergic. Wants to spend 11 minutes describing the trip and never think about logistics again until he's in the lounge.
  • High-trust if earned. Will give Tures his card, loyalty numbers, calendar — and fire it in 30 seconds if it fakes a confirmation or misses a non-stop he asked for.

The conversion thesis: Andy isn't a price shopper. He's a time-and-cognition shopper. Charge him for the time and he'll pay it.

Jobs to be done

Tures is hired to do five things — in priority order:

  1. Book exactly what I told you. If I name the hotel, you book that hotel. No "we found something close."
  2. Find the cleanest feasible alternative when my ask is impossible — and ask before committing. Default behavior on uncertainty: pause and ask, don't autopilot.
  3. Handle the chain of permissions. Use my Amex, credit my Finnair number, apply my status — and tell me what you did.
  4. Coordinate dinner reservations at the destinations — including the ones without a public API.
  5. Take care of stuff while I sleep. Schedule change, dinner cancellation, weather alert — Tures handles it and tells me what it did.

The jobs are explicitly not: inspire me with ideas (Andy already knows), find the cheapest deal (he has Kayak), or make pretty Instagram itineraries (he doesn't care).

Where Tures sits: the empty quadrant — executor-grade automation at concierge-grade taste. Faster than a human concierge, more trustable than AI research tools, more personal than Expedia, more transparent than any of them.

The defensibility isn't the LLM. It's the integration surface (Duffel + Booking partners + Resy + concierge inventory + card vault + loyalty graph) and the trust UX — the live agent dashboard that lets Andy see Tures reasoning before he believes it. Nobody else is building that.

Business model

Launch model: hybrid. $1,800/year subscription for unlimited trips + transparent supplier commissions disclosed in the receipt. Tures takes no hidden markup and may even save the user money vs. retail — predictable cost, aligned incentives.

The right initial tier slots between AmEx Platinum ($695/yr) and a working relationship with a top Virtuoso advisor.

Honest skepticism

  • Restaurant inventory at the top end is not solvable by software alone. Resy + OpenTable + SevenRooms cover maybe 60% of the urban dining graph. Be honest in the copy about what Tures can autobook vs. what it will request and confirm.
  • Anti-bot defenses on luxury hotel sites. Independent properties want direct bookings — that means browser automation against Cloudflare and friends. Plan for it; don't promise full coverage day one.
  • Card-on-file is PCI-DSS scope. Use a tokenization vault (Stripe-as-vault is almost certainly correct). A week-1 architectural decision.
  • Refunds and schedule changes are the hardest surface — not booking. Tures must monitor → propose → ask → execute. Skip "propose + ask" and you burn a customer in six weeks.
  • Trust collapse on a single visible failure. Never confirm anything you can't verify in two places (provider receipt + parsed email confirmation). No fake success states — ever.
  • The LLM never books anything itself. It produces a structured plan; a deterministic executor validates and submits. The model is the parser and explainer, not the booker.

What winning looks like in year 1

  • · 500 paying subscribers at $1,800/yr = $900K ARR
  • · 80%+ retention after the first trip
  • · NPS > 60, with 30%+ of signups from referrals
  • · Zero brand-killing confirmation failures. Acceptable: "we couldn't get this — here's why." Never: "we got it!" when we didn't.
